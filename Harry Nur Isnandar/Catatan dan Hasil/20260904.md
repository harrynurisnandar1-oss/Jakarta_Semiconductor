# Thevenin's Theorem and Norton’s Theorem

## Thevenin's Theorem 

basically A complex linear circuit can be replaced by a simple voltage source (VTHEV) in series with resistance (RTHEV).

<img width="700" height="300" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-04 075749" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/5aeab690-296c-4fd8-93e4-15171ddc4a94" />

Focus only on the two terminals of interest, A and F.

Treat the rest of the circuit as a “black box.” Any linear two-terminal circuit can be replaced by:

Thevenin voltage (VTHEV) — equivalent voltage source. the voltage at the *black box* terminals, which can be measured and calculated.

Thevenin resistance (RTHEV) — equivalent resistance in series. The resistor at the *black box* terminals, which can be measured and calculated.

In the field of engineering, the term "black box" is used to describe a system where only the inputs and outputs are known, while the internal structure or components remain hidden or intentionally ignored.

Here’s an example of how Thevenin’s theorem can be used in a voltage divider
circuit to easily calculate the voltage across and the current flow through an attached load.

1. First remove the load R3 and open up the terminals of interest (A and B). Then, determine the Thevenin voltage VTHEV using Ohm’s law or the voltage divider equation it’s the open- circuit voltage across A and B.
2. Next, calculate the Thevenin resistance RTHEV across the terminals A and B by replacing the dc source (VBAT) with a short and calculating or measuring the resistance across A and B. RTHEV is simply R1 and R2 in parallel.

## Norton’s Theorem
Thévenin's and Norton's theorems are essentially the same; the difference lies in how the current is calculated in Norton's theorem.

- R NORTON​ = R THEV
- V THEV = I NORTON R THEV
- I  NORTON = $\frac{R THEV}{V THEV}$ ​

<img width="844" height="363" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/38e02ec5-350c-4e3a-b034-443568cb004e" />

However, unlike the Thevenin voltage source connected in series with the Thevenin resistance, the Norton equivalent circuit consists of a current source connected in parallel with a resistor whose value is equal to that Thevenin resistance.

In essence, Norton's theorem relates to current sources just as Thevenin's theorem relates to voltage sources.
